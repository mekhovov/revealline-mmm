# Round 03 — Four focused families, twelve visual treatments

Updated 12 September 2026. This supersedes the broad theme recommendation in round 02. The earlier ocean, fantasy, and space concepts remain archived exploration; the active scope is the four families the user listed. These are proposed treatments for one shared Xonix-style game, not twelve separate products or a commitment to implement twelve modes.

## Priority and common identity

**The main game is a Ukrainian FPV arcade game set in the Russian–Ukrainian war.** Its revealed illustrations must visibly contain fictional, stylized Russian invading military forces: soldiers, military vehicles, hostile drones, and occupied military scenes. Ukrainian landscapes supply the setting and emotional meaning. They do not replace the requested enemy imagery.

The core action is always recognizable: leave a safe edge, draw a vulnerable line, avoid active threats, reconnect, and claim an area that reveals part of a picture. Enemy movement and challenges are invented arcade rules. Illustrations use fictional locations and compositions. This is an arcade fantasy, not a reconstruction of real operations.

A useful main-theme reveal sequence is **hidden image → exposed invading military scene → completed collectible scene**. A separate optional before/after reward may show recovery following mission completion, but the player should genuinely discover the military scene during play. Any units drawn into the background must be visually distinct from bright, animated hazards that can actually hit the player or trail.

Recommended next close-ups: **W1 Daybreak Front as the main direction; W2 Night Signal as a lighting comparison; C1 Living Ornament, R2 Amiga Summer, and B2 Navi Network as proof that the common visual system supports the other families.**

## Family 1 — Ukrainian FPV / Russian–Ukrainian war — MAIN

### W1 — DAYBREAK FRONT — strongest primary direction

**Visual:** painterly-looking pixel clusters, pale morning sky, sunflower gold, deep blue shadow, chalk-white drone highlights, rust-red enemy accents. Rural roads, fields, small buildings, damaged industrial edges, and recognizable Ukrainian details create place. Reserve the clearest lines and strongest contrast for the actual game board.

**Avatar and enemies:** a memorable FPV drone with a compact silhouette and Ukrainian blue/yellow identity. Active enemies are stylized hostile drones, angular perimeter patrols, and abstract interference creatures. Revealed artwork explicitly shows Russian invading soldiers, trucks, and armored vehicles with readable faction cues. Unit designs and scenes are fictional.

**Reward and twist:** a successful cut exposes another striking section of the occupied scene; large enclosures produce a stronger signal-lock animation and image discovery. Optional challenge symbols reward enclosing marked objectives in addition to reaching the capture percentage. Keep the starting rules simple.

**Music:** original tracker breakbeats, bandura-like plucks, controlled analog bass, and a hopeful melodic resolution. The music supports tension without using real combat radio or distress recordings.

**Best display:** desktop and handheld landscape, with a separately composed portrait image. The marketing close-up should show the drone, one open cut, one dangerous active enemy, and clearly revealed invading military presence in the same frame.

### W2 — NIGHT SIGNAL

**Visual:** near-black blue, amber streetlights, cyan rain reflections, violet haze, restrained red warnings. A fictional Ukrainian urban/industrial district at night, with windows, warehouses, street wires, and masonry providing a convincing setting.

**Avatar and enemies:** a white-and-gold FPV silhouette; hostile drones, perimeter sparks, and slow sweeping interference bands. The uncovered picture shows an invading military column or occupation scene. Lighting and simple silhouettes keep the threat legible even on a phone.

**Reward and twist:** captured regions resolve from noisy darkness into luminous pixel illustration. An optional pulse cycle creates clearly signaled changes in arcade hazard speed. The visual effect must not hide enemy positions or alter the meaning of the controls.

**Music / display:** dark synthwave, sparse drum-machine beats, brief radio-like synthetic chirps; strongest on Steam Deck, desktop, and TV. [Katana ZERO](https://www.devolverdigital.com/games/katana-zero) and [Narita Boy](https://www.team17.com/games/narita-boy) are useful mood references for deliberate lighting and audiovisual identity, not sources of military mechanics.

### W3 — STEEL HORIZON

**Visual:** burnt orange, oxidized copper, petrol blue, concrete cream; bold mosaic-like scenery and large machinery silhouettes. Fictional factory yards and rail architecture make this visibly different from fields or neon streets.

**Avatar and enemies:** a compact Ukrainian FPV drone against chunky mechanical pursuers and geometric interference hazards. Revealed scenes visibly contain invading military vehicles and personnel among industrial structures.

**Reward and twist:** enclosed areas lock into a monumental panel; a complete level produces an animated war illustration with a short title card. Optional connected-objective scoring turns separate successful cuts into a readable sequence.

**Music / display:** metallic percussion, warm FM bass, and a compact recurring melody. Broad shapes make this a good TV style and a strong low-detail mobile alternative. The mosaic treatment is an original design proposal informed by Ukrainian monumental art, not a claim that this imagined wartime scene is a historical artwork. [Ukrainian Institute — Mykhaylo Boychuk](https://uaview.ui.org.ua/artist/Boichuk-Mykhaylo).

Authenticity is part of the creative direction:

- **C1 uses distinct regional chapters.** Petrykivka floral painting, Kosiv ceramics, pysanka, embroidery, and Crimean Tatar Örnek retain their names and their own visual logic. A single board should follow one selected tradition instead of mixing every motif. [Petrykivka](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/petrykivka-decorative-painting-as-a-phenomenon-of-the-ukrainian-ornamental-folk-art-00893), [Kosiv](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/tradition-of-kosiv-painted-ceramics-01456?RL=01456), [Pysanka](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/pysanka-ukrainian-tradition-and-art-of-decorating-eggs-02134), [Örnek](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/ornek-a-crimean-tatar-ornament-and-knowledge-about-it-01601?RL=01601).
- **C2 uses an explicit era and source per pack.** Clothing, objects, buildings, language, and dates come from a selected collection or subject. Do not merge Kyivan Rus, Cossack life, and modern statehood into a supposedly accurate single scene. The National Museum's [digital collections](https://collection.nmiu.org/) and the Ivan Honchar Museum's [regional object catalogue](https://honchar.org.ua/collections/search) provide concrete starting references.
- **C3 includes Ukrainian modernity and creative authorship.** Ukraine's cultural identity extends into modern art and design. The Ukrainian Institute's [UA View](https://uaview.ui.org.ua/) presents Ukrainian artists and their contributions across places and periods; use specific named references when developing final packs.
- **Music carries provenance too.** A new tracker piece with a bandura-like sound is a contemporary interpretation. If adapting an actual traditional song or instrument practice, identify the source, arrangement, and performers. UNESCO documents both [Cossack song traditions](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/cossack-s-songs-of-dnipropetrovsk-region-01194) and [kobza/wheel-lyre practice](https://ich.unesco.org/en/BSP/safeguarding-programme-of-kobza-and-wheel-lyre-tradition-02136).

## One coherent pixel standard

| Shared element | Proposed standard across all four families |
| --- | --- |
| Pixel craft | One consistent source grid for gameplay sprites; deliberate clusters and stepped edges; no arbitrary mixing of smooth vector characters with pixel hazards |
| Sprite scale | Common avatar/enemy size tokens, initially exploring roughly 24–32 source-pixel avatar silhouettes and clearly differentiated enemy sizes |
| Hierarchy | The open player trail and active hazards are the most readable moving marks; decorative illustration sits at a quieter contrast level |
| Meaning | Safe border, open line, enemy, warning, objective, and completed capture retain the same semantic roles across themes |
| Palette | Small authored ramps per theme; shape and pattern reinforce status so meaning does not depend only on hue |
| Animation | A consistent movement rhythm and equivalent anticipation/capture/failure timing; each theme changes the material and musical response |
| UI | Pixel display lettering for short headings and numbers; a readable companion face for settings, history text, and accessibility |
| Viewports | Separate composed portrait and landscape artwork, common gameplay meaning, controls kept outside the critical board region |
| Effects | Pre-rendered lighting and selective animation first; optional restrained glow/CRT effects must preserve legibility |
| Review | Compare identical board state in every treatment, including a phone-size frame and one crowded enemy moment |

The first production art test should prove the main war family: one player sprite, three clearly distinct active threats, one military image reveal, a capture animation, compact HUD, portrait and landscape compositions, and matching sound cues. Once that works, the same board can be dressed in C1, R2, and B2 to prove that the framework's identity survives changing content.
